✦ A SMALL VOYAGE INTO SOMETHING IMMENSE

Make yourself
at home in space.

Step into a world.

Choose “Enter this world” for a field expedition. Use WASD to move, drag or arrow keys to look, E / Q to rise and lower, and Shift to cruise. Walk on solid ground and use Space to hop. “Follow a signal” offers an assisted flight. Escape returns to orbit.

Slip beneath the waves.

On Earth, descend straight into the ocean or choose “Dive into the sea.” Swim at the same speed as flight, following your gaze. Rise with E or the on-screen arrows to surface. Select After Dark before entering for a moonlit coast, and follow “Lanterns in the current” to meet the crystal jellies. Sound is optional.

Go around. Get closer.

Drag to orbit. Scroll or pinch to approach. On a keyboard, use the arrow keys to orbit and + / − to zoom.

Let curiosity lead.

Follow the glowing markers or choose “Look a little closer.” Every observation finds a home in your journal.

A change of perspective.

Choose a destination below, or open the solar map. Press F for a quiet view, R to reset, and Space to pause the drift.

Field expeditions are representative environments informed by the supplied research report and its scientific references. Local terrain, vegetation, clouds, and solar structures are procedural interpretations, not georeferenced reconstructions. Each environment has its own evidence panel. The Sun has no solid landing surface; Mercury has no ordinary atmospheric flight. Fictional protected observers make otherwise inaccessible environments explorable.

Earth’s sea is an imagined temperate Pacific habitat. Northern anchovies and crystal jellies appear at representative sizes. Brief jelly-rim flashes illustrate a disturbance response. After Dark uses a composed moonlit sky, with an optional observer light underwater. Water clarity, animal motion, and the synthesized ocean soundscape are interpretations.

This is an exploratory portrait of our solar neighborhood. Body sizes, orbital distances, travel times, and rotation speeds are adjusted for a comfortable journey. Positions are composed, not live ephemerides. Lighting is arranged for viewing; the starfield is illustrative.

Discovery markers introduce whole-world themes, not precise geographic locations. The Sun’s warm color and moving texture are an artistic interpretation. Venus’ surface view is a colorized, radar-derived map beneath an illustrated cloud layer.

Facts are rounded and checked against NASA’s Sun, Mercury, Venus, and Earth references. “Rotation” means a turn relative to the stars, not a sunrise-to-sunrise solar day.

Planet maps by Solar System Scope, based on NASA imagery, used under CC BY 4.0. Maps are rendered with custom lighting and color treatment. The optional ambient sound is a composed soundscape; sound does not travel through the vacuum of space.

Ground detail uses Rocky Terrain 02 by Amal Kumar / Poly Haven, released under CC0. This terrestrial material is rescaled and recolored for artistic detail; it is not measured rock imagery from Mercury or Venus.
